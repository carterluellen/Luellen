    <div class="publication-topline"><span>01 · GASTRIC ORGANOID MODEL</span><span>SEPTEMBER 2026</span></div>
    <h3>H. pylori infection does not increase matrix metalloproteinase activity of human gastric organoids</h3>
    <p class="authors">Catherine Azkue Acosta, Carter Luellen, Jessica Rua, Andrew T. Sebrell, and Diane Bimzok</p>
    <p class="mentor">Mentor: Michelle D. Cherne, Ph.D.</p>
    <div class="abstract">
      <p><strong>Background.</strong> <em>H. pylori</em> is a Gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa and infects approximately half of the world’s population. In chronic infection, persistent epithelial inflammation can induce matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), zinc-dependent enzymes involved in gastric extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. This study investigated the effect of <em>H. pylori</em> infection on MMP activity in human gastric organoids and evaluated experimentally induced collagen damage using fluorescein-labeled collagen hybridizing peptide (F-CHP) detection.</p>
      <p><strong>Methods.</strong> Four independent human gastric organoid samples were collected and treated on separate days per experimental condition: <em>H. pylori</em> microinjection, TNF-α treatment as a positive inflammatory control, and uninoculated microinjection as a mock mechanical-injury control. Samples were concentrated fourfold using centrifugal filter units. Each sample was plated in duplicate and incubated with APMA to activate pro-MMPs. MMP activity was measured using the Amplite Universal MMP Activity Assay Kit, with fluorescence measured kinetically over 60 minutes. Gastric tissue samples were separately treated with collagenase or heat denaturation; untreated tissue and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) served as controls. Collagen damage was evaluated using the 3Helix F-CHP assay.</p>
      <p><strong>Results.</strong> MMP activity did not significantly differ between the <em>H. pylori</em>-infected and mock-injection conditions (27.90 ± 0.66 vs. 31.96 ± 6.33 million RFU; p = 0.3968). The TNF-α condition exhibited greater MMP activity than the <em>H. pylori</em>-infected condition (37.75 ± 3.52 vs. 27.90 ± 0.66 million RFU; p = 0.0221). MMP activity did not significantly differ between TNF-α and mock-injection conditions (p = 0.1808). F-CHP fluorescence was highest in collagenase-treated tissue (83.59 ± 4.54 million RFU), followed by heat-denatured tissue (51.13 ± 2.06 million RFU) and untreated control tissue (18.56 ± 0.69 million RFU). PBS demonstrated minimal fluorescence.</p>
      <p><strong>Discussion.</strong> In this study, <em>H. pylori</em> infection did not increase MMP activity relative to mock-injected controls, while the TNF-α positive inflammatory control demonstrated greater MMP activity than <em>H. pylori</em>-infected samples. This may suggest distinct effects of bacterial and inflammatory signaling on MMP activity. The F-CHP assay detected collagen damage in positive-control gastric tissue, with collagenase-treated samples displaying the highest observed fluorescence. Future studies could examine additional inflammatory cytokines alongside <em>H. pylori</em> to distinguish bacterial from inflammatory effects on MMP activity.</p>
    </div>

    <div class="publication-topline"><span>02 · EXTRACELLULAR MATRIX</span><span>SEPTEMBER 2026</span></div>
    <h3>Microarchitecture of a human gastric extracellular matrix scaffold determined by scanning electron microscopy</h3>
    <p class="authors">Eli Mathew, Carter Luellen, Ava Richard, and Jessica Rua</p>
    <p class="mentor">Mentor: Michelle D. Cherne, Ph.D.</p>
    <div class="abstract">
      <p><strong>Objective.</strong> Compare structural differences between Matrigel and human gastric extracellular matrix (hgECM) using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and determine whether enzymatic degradation can be detected and quantified by measuring Matrigel pore size and hgECM fiber diameter.</p>
      <p><strong>Methods.</strong> Human gastric extracellular matrix was decellularized to produce scaffolds for visualization of gastric architecture under SEM. To investigate the effects of ECM proteases on microarchitecture, collagen was treated with collagenase and Matrigel was treated with trypsin. Decellularized hgECM and Matrigel samples underwent experimental enzyme degradation. Collagenase (2 mg/mL) was added to hgECM for 60 or 90 seconds, while Matrigel samples were trypsinized for 90 seconds or 10 minutes. Following enzymatic treatment, samples were fixed with formalin, dehydrated through graded ethanol, critical-point dried, and sputter coated. Samples were imaged using a JEOL JSM-IT710HR field-emission scanning electron microscope. Structural characteristics—including fiber organization, surface morphology, and pore sizes—were evaluated. Matrigel pore size and hgECM fiber diameter were quantified from SEM images using ImageJ with the Fiji extension.</p>
      <p><strong>Results.</strong> SEM demonstrated distinct microstructural differences between Matrigel and hgECM. Matrigel predominantly exhibited a porous structure, while hgECM was fibrous with a fragmented appearance. Mean Matrigel pore size was 1.351 ± 0.585 μm in negative controls, 1.051 ± 0.287 μm following 90 seconds of trypsinization, and 1.335 ± 0.462 μm following 10 minutes of trypsinization. There was no progressive reduction in pore size with increasing trypsin exposure. Mean hgECM fiber diameter was 0.139 μm in negative controls, 0.084 μm following 60 seconds of collagenase, and 0.108 μm following 90 seconds of collagenase. Collagenase-treated fiber diameters were smaller than the untreated mean.</p>
      <p><strong>Conclusions.</strong> Human gastric extracellular matrix displayed a disorganized fibrous microstructure composed primarily of collagen, distinct from laminin-rich Matrigel. Enzymatic degradation produced measurable changes in Matrigel pore size and hgECM fiber diameter that were detected using SEM. These findings provide baseline evidence supporting the use of SEM to evaluate structural remodeling of ECM scaffolds. Future studies are needed to investigate whether similar patterns of enzymatic degradation can be identified in gastric ECM following inflammatory or pathogen-mediated responses.</p>
    </div>
